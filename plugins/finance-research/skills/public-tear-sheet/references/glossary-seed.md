# Glossary seed

The Glossary tab includes every financial or filing term on the Overview or Tear sheet that a newcomer to investing wouldn't already know. Leave out everyday words and company-specific names. Add a few related terms if they help read the ones used. Typically that's 25–40 entries. Start from these entries. For each included term, add a final **Here** bullet giving this company's actual figure and, where it applies, the arithmetic, taken from the tear sheet. When the tear sheet uses a term not listed here, write a new entry in the same format and add it.

Format per entry (alphabetical on the tab):

```markdown
## Term (abbreviation)

Plain definition.

- *How it's calculated:* …
- *How to read it:* what's typical and what high or low signals.
- *Here:* company-specific example.
```

Write each entry as Markdown in this shape: the term as a heading of its own (`##` on the Glossary tab, under its title), the definition as a paragraph, then a bullet for each of *How it's calculated*, *How to read it* and *Here* that applies. The same shape renders in a doc, in the Markdown files and in a Word or PDF export; an entry run together on one line with inline `**` and `*` is what shows up as raw Markdown when it lands as text.

## Entries

### 52-week range

The lowest and highest share price over the past year.

- *How to read it:* shows where today's price sits relative to recent history. Near the low can mean bad news or a bargain, and the range alone doesn't say which.

### Analyst consensus (consensus estimate)

The average of forecasts published by Wall Street analysts who cover the company.

- *How to read it:* it's what the market roughly expects. Results above it often lift the stock, below it often drop it. Different data providers count different analysts, so averages vary. It is not the company's own forecast, which is called Guidance.

### Balance sheet

A snapshot of what a company owns (assets), owes (liabilities), and the difference (shareholders' equity) on one date.

- *How to read it:* look at cash versus debt first.

### Bear case / bull case

The strongest argument that the stock will do badly or well.

- *How to read it:* a fair picture needs both, and the bear case is where the risks get specific.

### Capex (capital expenditure)

Cash spent on long-lived things like buildings, equipment and data centers.

- *How to read it:* high capex is investment for the future, but it reduces free cash flow today.

### Cash flow from operations (operating cash flow, OCF)

Cash actually generated by running the business in a period.

- *How to read it:* harder to flatter than profit. If it's consistently far below net income, ask why.

### Catalyst

A scheduled or expected event that could move the stock: earnings, a product launch, a court ruling.

### Comparable sales (comps, same-store sales)

Sales growth at stores or units open at least a year, which removes the effect of opening new ones.

- *How to read it:* retail's purest measure of demand. Watch for distortions like fuel prices or currency.

### Diluted EPS (earnings per share)

Net income divided by the number of shares, counting shares that options and convertibles could create.

- *How it's calculated:* net income ÷ diluted shares.
- *How to read it:* the "E" in P/E.

### Drawdown

A fall in the share price from a previous peak, in percent.

- *How it's calculated:* (trough − peak) ÷ peak, from month-end closes on the Price chart tab.
- *How to read it:* the largest drawdown shows the worst ride a holder actually had, and how long the price took to recover shows how long they waited.

### Dual-class shares

Two kinds of stock with different voting power, often so founders keep control.

- *How to read it:* limits outside shareholders' influence and makes takeovers harder.

### EBITDA

Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. A rough measure of operating cash profit.

- *How to read it:* useful for comparing companies with different debt and accounting, but it ignores real costs like equipment wearing out. "Adjusted EBITDA" is the company's own version, with more items excluded.

### Enterprise value (EV)

What it would cost to buy the whole company, i.e. its stock plus its debt, minus the cash you'd get with it.

- *How it's calculated:* market cap + debt − cash.
- *How to read it:* better than market cap for comparing companies with different amounts of debt.

### EV / revenue

Enterprise value divided by a year's revenue.

- *How to read it:* used when profits are small, negative or distorted. Fast-growing software often trades at 5–15×, retailers often under 1×.

### Fiscal year (FY)

The company's own 12-month accounting year, which often isn't the calendar year.

- *How to read it:* always check which months "FY27" covers before comparing companies.

### Form 10-K / 10-Q / 8-K

US filings with the SEC. The 10-K is the annual report, audited. The 10-Q is quarterly, reviewed but not audited. The 8-K reports major events, and earnings releases are attached to one.

### Free cash flow (FCF)

Cash from operations minus capex. Cash the company could return to shareholders, use to pay debt, or invest.

- *How it's calculated:* OCF − capex.

### GAAP / non-GAAP

GAAP are the standard US accounting rules. Non-GAAP figures are the company's own adjusted versions, usually excluding items like stock compensation or acquisition costs.

- *How to read it:* non-GAAP can be more representative or more flattering, so read both and see what's excluded.

### Gross margin

The share of each sales dollar left after the direct cost of providing the product.

- *How it's calculated:* (revenue − cost of revenue) ÷ revenue.
- *How to read it:* software is often 70–80%+, retailers 10–15%.

### Guidance

The company's own forecast for coming periods.

- *How to read it:* management usually sets it to be beatable. Changes to guidance often move the stock more than the results themselves.

### Impairment

A write-down when something the company bought (often an acquisition) is judged to be worth less than it paid.

- *How to read it:* a non-cash loss that admits overpaying. It doesn't use cash in the period.

### IPO (initial public offering)

When a private company first sells shares to the public on a stock exchange.

### ISO (incentive stock option)

An employee stock option with special US tax treatment. Exercising it can trigger alternative minimum tax (AMT), and it usually must be exercised within a limited time after leaving the company.

- *How to read it:* the tax rules are complex, so get professional tax advice.

### Market cap (market capitalization)

The total value of all a company's shares at today's price.

- *How it's calculated:* share price × shares outstanding.

### Net bookings / ARR / GMV (company-specific sales measures)

Non-GAAP measures some companies use instead of revenue. Net bookings are sales agreed in the period. ARR is annual recurring revenue. GMV is the total value of goods sold through a platform.

- *How to read it:* check the company's definition. They can differ a lot from GAAP revenue.

### Net cash / net debt

Cash minus debt, or debt minus cash.

- *How to read it:* net cash is a cushion. Heavy net debt adds risk if business weakens.

### Operating income / operating margin

Profit from the main business before interest and taxes, and that profit as a share of revenue.

### P/E (price-to-earnings ratio)

Share price divided by earnings per share. How many years of current profit the price represents.

- *How to read it:* mature companies are often 15–25×. Higher means investors expect growth. Meaningless when earnings are tiny or negative. "Forward P/E" uses next year's expected earnings.

### Peer / comparable company (comps)

A similar listed company used as a yardstick for valuation.

### Post-money valuation

A private company's value right after a funding round, implied by the price investors paid.

- *How to read it:* set by a few investors in a negotiation, often with special protections, so it's not the same as a market price.

### Proxy statement (DEF 14A)

The annual filing for shareholder votes. It shows executive pay, the board, and who owns large stakes.

### Recurrent consumer spending

Take-Two's term for ongoing in-game spending (virtual currency, add-ons) rather than game purchases.

### Revenue (sales, top line)

Money earned from customers in a period, before costs.

### Run-rate

A recent month's or quarter's revenue multiplied up to a year.

- *How to read it:* flatters fast growers and seasonal businesses. For private companies it's usually an unaudited claim.

### Segment

A business division the company reports separately (e.g. by product or region).

### Share buyback

A company buying its own stock, reducing the number of shares.

### Takeover / take-private

A buyer acquires all of a company's shares. Take-private means a public company becomes private.

### Unaudited

Figures no independent auditor has checked, such as a private company's own claims.

### Valuation multiple

Any ratio of price or value to a business measure (P/E, EV/revenue, EV/EBITDA).

- *How to read it:* only meaningful compared with similar companies or the same company's history.

### Year over year (YoY)

Compared with the same period one year earlier. Removes seasonal effects.

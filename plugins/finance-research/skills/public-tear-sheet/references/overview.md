# Overview tab

## Purpose
The Overview is an on-ramp for a reader who isn't familiar with stocks or financial statements. It doesn't simplify by hiding numbers. It shows the *same* key numbers as the tear sheet, says in plain words what each one means, and points to where it sits on the tear sheet, so that reading the overview teaches the reader to read the real page. Someone who reads a few of these should start to find the tear sheet itself readable.

Write it last, from the finished tear sheet, so every number matches exactly. "Matches" means the same value. Adding a unit the table header carries is fine (a "$M" table's 461.5 becomes $461.5M), but rounding differently or recomputing is not.

**Length:** 500–750 words (private companies: up to 800). A translated overview listed under "Defaults for this installation" follows its own reference file for length and style.

## Structure (English Overview tab)
Open with a 2–3 sentence summary of the company and its situation right now, in everyday words. Then answer five questions. For each question, give:
- a plain answer containing the real number(s) from the tear sheet, written as on the tear sheet ($38.0B, 18%);
- **How to read it:** 1–3 sentences on what the number measures, how it's calculated in simple terms, and what's ordinary versus unusual for this kind of company ("most large, steady companies trade at 15–25× earnings; 40× means investors expect fast growth");
- **On the tear sheet:** where to find it ("Snapshot table, row 'P/E'").

The five questions (adapt the wording to the company; for a private company, see below):
1. **What does the company do, and who pays it?** Products, customers, and how it earns money: one-time sales, subscriptions, in-app purchases, fees.
2. **Is it growing?** Revenue (or the company's main sales measure) this year versus last year, as a percentage.
3. **Is it making money?** Profit or loss, and cash generated. If profit and cash disagree, explain why in a sentence (e.g. amortization of an acquisition is a cost on paper, not cash spent).
4. **What is it worth, and is that expensive?** Market value (market cap), and one valuation ratio compared with similar companies. Say what makes the ratio high or low.
5. **What could change the picture?** The one or two upcoming events and the main risk, with dates.

Bold each financial term on first use, and only financial terms (not company, product or person names). Every bolded term must have an entry on the Glossary tab.

**Private companies:** replace questions 2–4 with:
- *How big is it, and how do we know?* Company claims, with dates, and why they're unaudited.
- *What have investors paid for it?* Funding rounds and valuation, and what a valuation in a funding round means.
- *Why does it matter to public companies?* The read-through in plain words.

Where public data is thin, say so plainly. That's a lesson in itself.

## Simplify without drifting
Plain language is where facts quietly change. Check each overview sentence against the tear sheet for these:
- **Time-sensitive figures keep their date.** Odds, prices, forecasts and consensus carry "as of" in the text ("a prediction market priced ~9% odds of a delay on Sep 10"). Never present an old reading in the present tense.
- **Qualifiers survive.** "Only on consoles at launch; PC version unscheduled" must not become "console-only". The same goes for "reportedly", "expected", "company-claimed", "about" and "so far".
- **Attribution stays attribution.** If a source attributes a move to something, say "one analysis attributes…". Don't promote it to a cause ("because investors worry…").
- **Same word, same measure.** When the tear sheet has two forecasts (net bookings guidance and revenue guidance), name each one distinctly every time, in every language.

## Tone
Write as a knowledgeable friend explaining over coffee, not a textbook. Short sentences, concrete comparisons, no condescension. Assume an intelligent adult who is new to the vocabulary, not new to thinking. Never give advice on what to do with the stock.

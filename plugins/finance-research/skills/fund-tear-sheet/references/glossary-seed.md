# Glossary seed (funds)

The Glossary tab includes every term on the Overview or Tear sheet that a newcomer to investing wouldn't already know — skip everyday words and fund/issuer names — typically 20–35 entries. Start from these; for each included term, add a final **"Here:"** line with this fund's actual figure from the tear sheet. When the tear sheet uses a term not listed, write a new entry in the same format.

Format: **Term** (abbreviation): plain definition. *How it's calculated:* … *How to read it:* … *Here:* …

---

**12b-1 fee**: an annual marketing/distribution charge some mutual funds include in their expense ratio. *How to read it:* index funds and most no-load funds have none.

**Active share**: the percentage of a fund's holdings that differs from its benchmark. *How to read it:* below ~60% suggests a fund that mostly mirrors its index while charging active fees ("closet indexing").

**Active vs passive (index) fund**: an active fund's managers choose what to buy; a passive fund copies an index by rule. *How to read it:* most active funds trail their index after fees over long periods; the question for any active fund is whether its record and fee justify the difference.

**Asset allocation**: the split of a portfolio among stocks, bonds and cash.

**Benchmark**: the index a fund is measured against. *How to read it:* a fair comparison uses the same period and total returns.

**Capital-gains distribution**: profits a fund realizes from selling holdings, paid out to shareholders — taxable that year in a taxable account even if reinvested. *How to read it:* ETFs and index funds usually distribute little; high-turnover active funds can distribute a lot.

**Category**: a peer group of similar funds (e.g. "Large Growth") used for comparisons, typically Morningstar's.

**Closet indexing**: an active fund that holds nearly the same things as its index while charging more.

**Concentration** / **top-10 share**: how much of the fund sits in its largest holdings. *How it's calculated:* sum of the top 10 weights. *How to read it:* a broad US index fund is ~30–40% in its top 10 today; above ~50% means a few companies drive results.

**Distribution yield**: the last year's (or annualized recent) payouts divided by price. *How to read it:* not standardized; compare funds using SEC yield instead.

**Drawdown**: a fall from a previous peak, in percent. *How to read it:* the maximum drawdown shows the worst ride an investor actually had.

**Duration**: a bond fund's sensitivity to interest rates, in years. *How to read it:* price change ≈ −duration × change in rates; duration 6 means about −6% if rates rise 1 percentage point.

**Effective number of holdings**: how many equally-weighted holdings would give the same concentration. *How it's calculated:* 1 ÷ sum of squared weights. *How to read it:* a fund with 100 holdings can behave like one with 30.

**ETF (exchange-traded fund)**: a fund that trades on an exchange all day like a stock. *How to read it:* usually lower cost and more tax-efficient than a mutual fund holding the same things.

**Expense ratio** (gross / net): the annual cost of running the fund as a percentage of your investment, taken out of returns automatically. Net is after any temporary fee waiver. *How to read it:* 0.03–0.20% is typical for broad index funds; 0.50–1.00% for active funds. *Here:* also in dollars per $10,000.

**Fee drag**: how much an expense ratio reduces an investment's value over time compared with a cheaper fund. *How it's calculated:* by fund_calc, compounding both at the same assumed return.

**Fee waiver**: a temporary reduction of fees by the issuer, with an end date in the prospectus.

**Glide path**: how a target-date fund's mix shifts from stocks toward bonds as the target year approaches and passes.

**Index**: a rule-defined list of securities with weights (e.g. Nasdaq-100, S&P 500). The rules decide what's in and how much.

**Money market fund**: a mutual fund holding very short-term, high-quality debt, aiming to keep its price at $1.00 and pay interest. *How to read it:* not FDIC-insured; government money funds hold mainly Treasury bills, agency debt and repurchase agreements.

**Mutual fund**: a fund bought from and sold to the issuer once a day at the closing NAV.

**NAV (net asset value)**: the value of one fund share — holdings minus liabilities, divided by shares. *How to read it:* ETFs also trade at a market price that can differ slightly (premium/discount).

**Premium / discount**: how far an ETF's market price is above or below its NAV. *How to read it:* usually tiny (<0.1%) for large ETFs.

**Prospectus / summary prospectus**: the legal document describing a fund's objective, strategies, risks, fees and managers.

**Repurchase agreement (repo)**: a very short loan backed by securities like Treasuries; a large part of many money funds.

**SEC yield** (7-day, 30-day): a standardized yield formula set by the SEC — 7-day for money funds, 30-day for bond funds. *How to read it:* the fair basis for comparing yields between funds.

**Share class**: different versions of the same fund with different fees or minimums (e.g. Investor vs Admiral).

**Standard deviation** (volatility): how widely returns swing around their average. *How to read it:* higher means a bumpier ride.

**State-tax exemption (US government obligations)**: interest from US Treasury securities is exempt from state income tax; a fund reports what share of its income came from them. *How to read it:* matters most in high-tax states; repo income usually doesn't qualify, and some states have thresholds.

**Tax-advantaged account** (IRA, 401(k), Roth): an account where investment income isn't taxed year by year. *How to read it:* fund-level tax points such as state exemption, capital-gains distributions and ETF tax efficiency don't apply inside it. Traditional-account withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income; qualified Roth withdrawals are tax-free.

**Target-date fund**: an all-in-one fund for people retiring around a given year, holding other funds and following a glide path.

**Total return**: price change plus reinvested distributions. *How to read it:* the right measure for comparing funds.

**Total net assets** (AUM, assets under management): the total value of the fund (or of one share class).

**Tracking difference / tracking error**: how far an index fund's return differs from its index (difference = gap in return; error = how much the gap varies). *How to read it:* the difference is usually close to the expense ratio.

**Turnover**: the share of holdings replaced in a year. *How to read it:* index funds are often under 10%; high turnover means more trading costs and often more taxable distributions.

**Unit investment trust (UIT)**: an older fund structure with fixed rules — e.g. it can't lend securities or reinvest dividends before paying them out. SPY uses it; check the current prospectus for any given fund, since some have converted.

**Volatility decay**: why a daily-leveraged fund loses value in choppy markets even if the index ends flat.

**WAM / WAL** (weighted average maturity / life): how soon, on average, a money fund's holdings mature, in days. *How to read it:* shorter means the yield follows rate changes faster; SEC rules cap WAM at 60 days and WAL at 120.

**Yield to maturity**: the return a bond fund's holdings would earn if held to maturity at today's prices, before fees.
